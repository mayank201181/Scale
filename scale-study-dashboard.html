Everything is set by size.

And the relationship between size and almost everything else is a power law — a single clean number, the exponent, that both falls out of physical structure and turns around to predict the world.

Start here — the one equation

A power law is y = k·xᵃ

On ordinary axes it's a curve. Take the log of both sides and it becomes a straight line whose slope is the exponent a. The whole book lives on that line. Drag the exponent and watch what a actually means.

The move that unlocks the book: read the exponent as order-of-magnitude in Y = a × order-of-magnitude in X. Multiply size by 100 (two orders) and output multiplies by 32 — that's what a buys you.

The defining property

Scale invariance

Multiply the input by any factor, at any scale, and the output multiplies by a fixed factor — no matter where you started.

This is the property that makes a power law special, and it is not shared by exponentials.

"If the mass is increased by any arbitrary factor at any scale (100, in the example), then the metabolic rate increases by the same factor (32…) no matter what the value of the initial mass is — whether it's that of a mouse, cat, cow, or whale."
Scale, p. 92

Power law ≠ exponential

Compounding is exponential. At 10% money doubles every ~7 years. Multiply the input and the answer changes depending on where you began:

7→14 yr
2× becomes 4×  ·  a factor of 2
14→28 yr
4× becomes 16×  ·  a factor of 4

Same multiplication of the input, different answer. A power law never does that — the factor is locked, which is exactly why it can predict.

Reference — the exponent zoo

One number tells you the sign of the whole system

Below 1, output grows slower than size → economies of scale, a system that slows down and stops. Above 1, output grows faster than size → acceleration, open-ended growth toward a singularity. The pivot is exactly 1.

The idea running through the whole book

The two-way street

The exponent is a hinge. Physical reality determines it going one way; it predicts reality going the other. The most compelling laws do both.

DIRECTION 1

Physical reality → the exponent

Constraints — how fluid moves through a tube, how surface area lags volume, how a network must reach every cell — force the exponent into existence. The 3/4 in Kleiber's law isn't fitted; it's determined by a fractal network filling a 3D body.

When you have this, you get something close to a real theory — biology's networks.
DIRECTION 2

The exponent → predicting reality

Once it exists, run it backwards. Ignore a city's history, mayor and culture; feed in its population and 1.15 predicts wages, patents, crime and road length — a strong forecast, independent of idiosyncrasy.

Sometimes you get only this: a predictive regularity floating free of its mechanism — markets, coastlines.
Why it matters: Rayleigh predicts the blue sky and Mandelbrot measures a coastline's crinkliness — both without a mechanism. "We know the scaling properties, which can allow us to handicap a prediction, but we don't know the mechanics." (p. 78). The thrill of the book is watching messy reality collapse into one number, then watching that number turn around and predict the world.
Deep dive · sublinear biology

Kleiber's law and the magic number 4

Metabolic rate scales as mass3/4 — and quarter-powers show up across 50+ biological laws, holding across 27 orders of magnitude, in mammals, birds, fish, plants and cells alike.

The elephant vs the rat is the canonical picture: ~10,000× the mass (4 orders), but only ~1,000× the energy (3 orders). A ratio of 3 to 4 in the powers of ten.

+75%
energy needed when you double an animal's size — not +100%. It saves 25% per doubling.
~1/10
the rate an elephant's cells run vs a rat's — why big animals live longer, mature slower, beat their hearts slower.
"Small creatures live life in the fast lane while large ones move ponderously, though more efficiently, through life."
Scale, p. 28

Double a mammal's mass and every timescale (lifespan, maturity) stretches ~25%, while every rate (heart rate) drops ~25%. The pace of life slows systematically with size.

Why 3/4 and not 2/3? Because 4 = 3 + 1

A smooth 3D object's exchange surface scales at 2/3 of volume. But a network optimized to be maximally space-filling crinkles its surfaces until they behave like a higher dimension — gaining one effective dimension.

4 = 3 space + 1 fractal
"Fractal geometry has literally given life an added dimension." — p. 154

The washing-machine intuition (p. 152)

Sheets are basically 2D. Double the machine's size and you'd expect 2² = 4× as many. But crumple them to fill the tub and you fit 2³ = 8× — they scale like a volume. The crumpling turned a 2D thing into a 3D-scaling one. That's exactly what a biological network does to its exchange surface.

The three postulates behind every quarter-power law (p. 284)

1 · Space-filling

The network must reach everywhere — "the tentacles of the network have to extend everywhere throughout the system it is serving" (p. 112). Every cell needs servicing.

2 · Invariant terminal units

Capillaries, mitochondria and cells are ~the same size in a mouse and a blue whale. Evolution reuses them and rescales the network above — like the outlets in a Shanghai tower matching the ones in your house.

3 · Optimization

Selection minimizes the energy the heart spends pumping, leaving the most for reproduction — the same least-action principle behind Newton, Maxwell and quantum mechanics. Nature minimizes.

Supporting hardware

The engineering that makes the network work

Area-preserving branching (p. 120). Daughter tubes' cross-sections sum to the parent's, so radii shrink by exactly √2 per branch. This kills wave reflections (impedance matching — same idea as loudspeakers, power grids and the bones in your middle ear). Da Vinci knew the tree version.

AC → DC in your arteries (p. 124). Blood leaves the heart as a pulsatile wave; viscous drag damps it to steady flow by the capillaries. That's why you feel a pulse only in big arteries.

Why mammals bottom and top out. Smallest — the Etruscan shrew (~2 g, heart 1,000+ bpm) — is set by where pulsatile waves can still propagate. Largest (~100 kg, blue-whale territory) by the Krogh radius: how far oxygen diffuses before cells starve. Godzilla fails both this and Galileo's strength limit.

The payoff — why growth stops

Death is a direct consequence of the 3/4 exponent

Double the size and cells double, so maintenance energy needed doubles — ×2. But supply only scales as 23/4 = 1.68. The surplus available for growth shrinks to zero, and you stop growing even while you keep eating.

"The increase in the number of supply units (the capillaries) cannot keep up with the demands from the increase in the number of customers (the cells)."
Scale, p. 166

The master dynamic: sublinear scaling → bounded, sigmoidal growth → a stable adult size → eventual death. Structure hands you the exponent; the exponent predicts the shape of a life.

Deep dive · the geometry underneath

Galileo's 2/3 — weight outruns strength

The original "physics produces an exponent" story, 400 years early. Scale a shape up by a linear factor L: area (and strength) grows as L², but volume (and weight) grows as L³. So strength scales as weight2/3, and the big get relatively weaker.

×1000
weight when a tree or tower is made 10× taller (10³)…
×100
…but only this much strength (10²). Safety margin drops to 1/10. (p. 42)
"A small dog could probably carry on his back two or three dogs of his own size; but I believe that a horse could not carry even one of his own size."
Galileo, quoted in Scale, p. 42

Confirmed by prediction running forward: Lietzke plotted 1956 Olympic weightlifting totals against body weight and got a slope of 0.675 — against the predicted 2/3 = 0.667. A clean hit.

One order of magnitude in area = 1.5 orders in volume/energy.

An area problem, not volume. Dosing the elephant Tusko by body weight — the LSD case — killed him. Right dose: milligrams.

BMI assumes weight ∝ height²; Galileo says height³. So it over-diagnoses tall people. The Ponderal index (weight/height³) has the right basis.
Deep dive · prediction without a mechanism

Fractals and the coastline problem

Richardson's accident: a coastline's measured length grows as your ruler gets finer, instead of converging. Plot length vs resolution on log-log → a straight line → a power law whose slope measures crinkliness.

Mandelbrot's fractal dimension = power-law slope + 1. Double the resolution and Britain's west coast grows ~25%; Norway's grows over 50% (p. 139). A line crinkly enough to fill an area has fractal dimension 2.

1.02
South African coast — nearly smooth
1.52
Norwegian coast — very crinkly

Mandelbrot had the measuring tool but showed little interest in why a coastline has its dimension. He had the ruler, not the mechanism. "You wanna make money or you wanna be right?"

Variability = resilience

Healthy hearts have high fractal dimension — spiky, variable EKGs. Dangerously sick hearts go smooth, dimension near 1. The capacity to fluctuate is what makes a system robust — a theme West extends to companies, cities and ecosystems.

Markets are fractal too: cotton-futures charts look statistically the same at an hour or five years — self-similar across timescales, the seed of econophysics. Predictive only in a coarse, average sense, never for a single stock.

Deep dive · superlinear cities

Cities flip biology's sign

Physical infrastructure still scales sublinearly (~0.85) for the same space-filling reason as biology. But socioeconomic output scales superlinearly (~1.15), driven by social networks rather than physical pipes. Where biology is bounded and slows, cities are open-ended and accelerate.

"Doubling the size of a city not only increases wages, wealth, and innovation by approximately 15 percent per capita but also increases the amount of crime, pollution, and disease to the same degree… the good, the bad, and the ugly come together hand in glove."
Scale, p. 277

Know a US city's size and you predict 80–90% of its wages, patents, road length, AIDS cases, violent crime and number of restaurants — independent of mayor, history or culture.

The mechanism, and the danger

The number of possible interactions among N people is ~N²/2. More people → disproportionately more connections → more output of every kind. The physical city fills space sublinearly; the social city connects superlinearly.

Superlinear scaling → faster-than-exponential (superexponential) growth → finite-time singularities. The engine of a city's open-ended growth is also what threatens to run it off a cliff.

Deep dive · companies sit in the middle

Companies behave like organisms, not cities

Most company metrics scale sublinearly — dominated by economies of scale, not innovation. Effective metabolic rate (sales vs employees) comes out roughly linear; expenses start sublinear then turn linear. Linear scaling mathematically yields the exponential growth every firm chases — but the organism-like dynamics mean most mature, stagnate and die.

"Half of all the companies in any given cohort of U.S. publicly traded companies disappear within ten years, and a scant few make it to fifty."
Scale, p. 33

Cities almost never die; companies and organisms do. The difference is diversity. Cities keep adding new kinds of jobs and businesses as they grow; companies narrow their product space and pile on bureaucracy.

Uncategorized gems

Four more patterns worth carrying around

Zipf's law rank⁻¹

Word frequency ∝ 1/rank; the top word appears ~2× as often as the 2nd, 3× as the 3rd. The very same form fits city-size distributions. Nobody fully agrees why it's so universal. (p. 310)

Mobility symmetry 1/(d·f)²

Visits obey an inverse-square law in distance × frequency. From 4 km once a month: 1,600 people; from 8 km: 400; from 20 km: 64. Hold distance × frequency constant and the count is constant. (p. 341)

Temperature is exponential

Metabolic rate rises exponentially with temperature, because reaction rates do. Every 10°C doubles the rate of living; a 2°C rise → 20–30% change in growth and mortality across all life. The climate-change kicker. (p. 175)

Dimensionless numbers

Ratios independent of your units (Froude v²/L, π, Reynolds-type). Match the dimensionless number and a model ship predicts the real ship — the whole basis of scale-model engineering. (pp. 74–77)

The skill the book is really teaching

Read every exponent as orders of magnitude

Take logs of y = xᵃ and it becomes log y = a · log x. Since a log just asks "what order of magnitude is this?", the exponent is a conversion rate between them:

orders of magnitude in Y = a × orders of magnitude in X

The options cousin of Kleiber's law

Option prices scale with time1/2 at constant vol. So a 1-month straddle worth $4 gives a 4-month straddle worth not 4× but 41/2 = 2× → $8. Sublinear scaling for a multiplicative factor.

Stretch time 100× (a 3-day vs a 1-year straddle): that's 2 orders in X, so only ½ × 2 = 1 order in Y. A name with a ~$20 one-year straddle has a ~$2 three-day straddle.

If options scaled by time3/4 instead of time1/2, prices would be wildly different — a whole extra order of magnitude. That's why a Hurst exponent of 0.5 is a completely different world from 0.6.

Why exponents trip us up

We handle linear relationships fluently — the grocery price is per pound, not per pound-squared — because we practice them constantly. Power, exponential and log intuition is just as important but far less rehearsed.

The villa test: a 500 m² villa at €1.5 M is €3,000/m². Guessing "≈€1,000/ft²" forgets that area converts by the linear factor squared: 1 m ≈ 3.28 ft, so 1 m² ≈ 10.8 ft², and the real figure is ~€280/ft². Trust the exponent, not the reflex.
