The framing — the book's duality, for a book

Two kinds of scaling knowledge

West's core tension: some exponents are a real theory (structure forces them, and they predict) — others are a regularity floating free of its mechanism ("we know the scaling properties which let us handicap a prediction, but we don't know the mechanics," p. 78). Both are usable at the desk; you just use them differently.

Tight law · the most profitable one to internalize

The square-root law of market impact

Push size into the market and the price moves against you by roughly impact ≈ σ·√(Q/ADV) — sublinear in your order (a ½ exponent, Kleiber's cousin). But your total execution cost is order × impact, so it scales as Q^1.5 — Galileo's 3/2, superlinear. The trap: cost per share keeps rising even though impact "feels" cheap.

Scale link: this is the book's clearest "prediction without mechanism" — a robust empirical √-law nobody fully derives (p. 78). Handicap with it anyway.

Desk action

Halving participation (slice the order, trade over 2 days) cuts impact by 1−1/√2 ≈ 29%. Because cost is Q^1.5, the last third of a big order is where the bleed lives — that's your capacity limit talking.

Tight law · options, vol, stops

Everything in vol scales as √time

Variance adds linearly across independent periods, so volatility, an ATM straddle, and your Sharpe annualization all scale as √T. This is the exact sibling of the metabolic ¾ law — a sublinear power that our linear intuition gets wrong every time.

1.0% per day
Desk action

A 4-month straddle is worth 2× a 1-month, not 4×. Quote and compare optionality per √T; size stops and targets in σ·√(holding days), never in linear days.

Tight law · which strategy the regime rewards

The Hurst exponent picks your game

The √T rule assumes a random walk (H = 0.5). Real paths deviate: range scales as Tᴴ. Below ½ the series mean-reverts (fade extremes); above ½ it trends (ride momentum). The essay's punchline — 0.5 vs 0.6 is a different world — is literally your strategy selection.

0.50
In practice H sits near 0.5, drifts with regime, and is noisy to estimate on short samples — treat it as a lean, not a signal on its own. Same caution West gives every free-floating exponent.
Tight law · the one that saves the fund

Fat tails & the coastline of your own P&L

Returns are power-law, not Gaussian — P(|r|>x) ∝ x⁻ᵅ, with equity α≈3. The Gaussian assumption that underwrites most risk systems dramatically underprices the tail. And the "coastline paradox" bites twice: the finer you sample the path, the more length (turnover, cost, noise) you measure.

Desk action

Size for the power-law tail, not the bell curve: cap single-name risk, own convex hedges that pay in the tail, and fractional-Kelly your sizing. On the coastline point — more screens ≠ more edge; finer sampling mostly buys you turnover and cost.

Prediction without mechanism

Mandelbrot's markets

Cotton-futures charts look statistically the same at an hour or five years — self-similar, quantified by an exponent, predictive only in a coarse average sense. Mandelbrot measured the crinkliness and never cared why. "You wanna make money or you wanna be right?"

The trap it warns against

Smooth models, jagged reality

A Gaussian VaR calls a yearly event a once-a-century one. The whole point of West's opening: scaling intuition is what protects you from a plausible-sounding narrative that's ignorant of how reality actually scales.

Tight law · sizing & attention

A few trades make the year — Zipf your own book

Just as word frequency and city sizes follow rank⁻¹, your P&L is radically concentrated: the top handful of positions carries most of the return. That has two hard consequences — don't clip your winners early (you're truncating the tail that pays you), and watch that the same concentration is also your risk.

Structural lens · the Kleiber's law of funds

Why alpha growth stops

In biology, growth halts because maintenance (cells) scales faster than supply (capillaries scale as size¾). In a fund, the same shape: gross edge per dollar is roughly flat, but impact cost per dollar rises with size (that √-law again). Net alpha climbs, peaks, and rolls over. Every strategy has a maximum size — and it's smaller than you'd like.

Scale link: "the increase in supply units cannot keep up with the demands from the increase in customers" (p. 166) — swap capillaries/cells for liquidity/AUM.

Desk action

Past the peak, taking more capital lowers total dollars earned. The organism-like move is to cap the strategy, return capital, or diversify into a second uncorrelated edge — the way a city keeps living by adding new industries.

Structural lens · survive to compound

Mortality, and why a smooth curve is a warning sign

Companies and strategies are organisms: they mature, stagnate and die — half of US public companies vanish within a decade (p. 33). And West's heart-rate finding flips a comforting intuition: a healthy heart is spiky (high fractal dimension); a dangerously sick one goes smooth. An equity curve that's too smooth is usually hiding a short-vol tail, not proving skill.

Desk action

Prize the capacity to fluctuate. Diversify edges, keep dry powder, and be suspicious of any book whose beauty comes from never having a red month — that's fractal-dimension≈1, the sick-heart signature. Base-rate your single-name longs against corporate mortality.

Productivity · think faster, transfer across assets

Dimensionless thinking & orders of magnitude

West's model ships predict real ships because a dimensionless number (Froude, Reynolds) transfers across scale. The desk version: strip units and compare setups by ratios that travel across assets and regimes — and reason in orders of magnitude to sanity-check any story in seconds.

Trade in dimensionless units

Ratios that transfer

  • Move sized in σ or ATR, not points — a 30bp move in bonds and a 3% move in a biotech can be the same 2σ event.
  • Order size in %ADV; edge in multiples of cost; carry in vol-adjusted terms.
  • Sharpe, Sortino, hit-rate×payoff — all unit-free, all comparable across your book.
The order-of-magnitude reflex

Log-first sanity checks

Before the model, ask "what order of magnitude should this be?" It kills bad narratives fast: a TAM that needs 3 orders of magnitude of adoption; a "10× the size, 10× the impact" claim (it's √, so ~3×); an annualized number someone scaled linearly from a day.

The rule: orders of magnitude in Y = a × orders of magnitude in X. Carry the exponent, not the anecdote.
